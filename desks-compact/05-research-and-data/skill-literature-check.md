# Literature check

Serves #49 (before the physical work, "check if someone else has already done this") and #47 (where new grain-storage research is published is open, so Chris names the places to look). Chris reviews it before it is used (the review step Justin's team recommends, #48). This desk checks the published record at a desk; the research itself is physical work, and it stays Chris's.

## Inputs
- Chris's question in one sentence: what he wants to find out, in which grain or storage type, under what conditions, in which region, measured how.
- The sources Chris names: journals, publisher or publication lists, websites, his own library. If he names none, ask for the three places he would look first.
- `owner.md`: the project's data rule (the lines "Research data in AI tools" and "Participant details in AI tools", ask #90).

## Steps
1. **Data rule first, then only the question goes out.** Look in `owner.md` for the project's rule. Whatever it says, the search gets only the question and its keywords. Never put project data, readings, participant details or unpublished results into a search or a page. If nothing is recorded, ask "Is AI allowed on <project>'s data?" and carry on with the question alone.
2. **Write the searches.** Two or three phrasings, with the Australian and international terms for the same thing. Note for each: where, the words used, and the date.
3. **Search in this order:** the sources Chris named, then the open web, then any publication lists he names. Say which sources you could not reach, such as paywalled or login-only.
4. **Open every candidate** before you cite it. Use only what you opened. If you read only an abstract, write "abstract only".
5. **One row per source,** with these columns:
   - Citation: authors, year, title, where published, link.
   - What was tested: conditions, and scale (lab, silo, farm).
   - What it found: a short quote from the source, with its section or page.
   - How close it is to Chris's question: same, close or different, and why.
   - Flags: small trial, not peer reviewed, old, a different climate, from a supplier.
6. **Give a verdict** in three words with the reasoning: "Already done", "Partly done" or "Not found". "Not found" means not found in the places searched. It never means that nothing exists.
7. **If partly or fully done:** what still hasn't been tested, and what Chris could reuse. The desk doesn't decide whether the physical work is still worth doing. That is Chris's call.
8. **Say what would change the answer:** a source you couldn't open, a term you didn't try, a place Chris could add.
9. **Add to the list.** If a new place turned out useful, suggest Chris adds it to the places he checks (#47). He decides.

## Output
One page: the question · the verdict · the sources table · where you searched, with search words and dates · what you couldn't open · what would change the answer · "Desk research, reviewed by Chris before use."

## Done when
Every cited source was opened, every finding is quoted or marked abstract-only, the search is listed so Chris can see the gaps, and Chris has the page for review.

## Never
- Cite a source you didn't open, or invent a title, link, number or quote.
- Present a paraphrase as a quote, or an unreviewed paper as settled.
- Say "no one has done this".
- Decide for Chris that work can be skipped.
- Send project data to a search or a page.
